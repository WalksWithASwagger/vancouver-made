# Subversive Soccer Kits: A Sourced Visual Reference Library

**Prepared for Kris Krüg — protest-kit design project, Vancouver hackathon**

## TL;DR

- This library maps four lineages of protest design — the body/uniform as protest surface; brand-hijack/subvertising; Vancouver street/skate space reclamation; and Indigenous-culture commodification fights — with named works, creators, years, source URLs, and design-grammar takeaways.
- The strongest, rights-aware provenance sits in museum and archive collections: the Smithsonian NMAAHC holds Tommie Smith’s actual 1968 tracksuit (Open Access) and a CC0 Kaepernick photograph; the City of Vancouver Archives holds China Creek skatepark slides; news agencies (AP, Getty/Popperfoto, AFP) hold the canonical photos.
- For a protest kit, the most transferable techniques are: hijacking official polish (Brandalism/Adbusters), loading garment details with coded meaning (1968 black socks/beads), and foregrounding the “who owns this culture” question (Cowichan sweater, Ilanaaq logo).

## Key Findings

- Athlete-protest imagery is overwhelmingly held by photo agencies (AP, Getty, AFP, Popperfoto) — meaning any reuse is a rights negotiation, which is itself thematically on-point for a project about who packages culture.
- Two artifacts of the 1968 protest are in the Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture, giving museum-grade provenance beyond the press photos — and several are marked Open Access/public domain.
- The subvertising lineage runs directly through Vancouver: Adbusters was founded there in 1989 by Kalle Lasn and Bill Schmalz, making it local heritage for this project.
- Both the 2010 Cowichan sweater and the Ilanaaq logo controversies are documented in academic and news sources and turn on the exact theme of the kit project: appropriation and commodification at mega-events.

## Details

### THREAD 1 — UNIFORM AS MESSAGE SURFACE

**1. Tommie Smith & John Carlos, 1968 Olympics Black Power salute (John Dominis / LIFE)**

- *Description:* Smith (gold) and Carlos (bronze) on the 200m podium, heads bowed, black-gloved fists raised during the US anthem, Mexico City, October 16, 1968. Silver medalist Peter Norman stands at left wearing an OPHR (Olympic Project for Human Rights) badge. Because Carlos forgot his gloves, Norman suggested they split Smith’s pair — which is why Carlos raised his left fist rather than his right.
- *Creator/year:* John Dominis, for LIFE, 1968.  Other canonical frames exist from AP and Rolls Press/Popperfoto.
- *Where to find it:* TIME/LIFE feature: <https://time.com/3880999/black-power-salute-tommie-smith-and-john-carlos-at-the-1968-olympics/> ; Getty Images (Rolls Press/Popperfoto and others): <https://www.gettyimages.com/photos/1968-olympics-black-power-salute> ; Wikipedia overview: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Olympics_Black_Power_salute>
- *Visual grammar:* Every element is a coded sign. Per NMAAHC sports curator Damion Thomas, “Carlos wore a necklace of beads to commemorate Black victims of racist violence and lynching… Smith and Carlos took their shoes off to illustrate poverty among Black Americans.” Add the black socks (poverty), Smith’s scarf (Black pride), Carlos’s unzipped jacket (blue-collar solidarity), and the single split glove (forced improvisation become symbol). Design lesson: a kit can carry an entire manifesto in accessory details, not slogans.

**2. Smith’s tracksuit & artifacts at the Smithsonian (NMAAHC)**

- *Description:* The actual gold-medal warm-up suit and associated objects. Per Smithsonian Magazine, NMAAHC acquired Smith’s tracksuit “along with the shoes he wore in that race, his official Olympic bag from the Games, and the box containing the olive tree branch he held as he stood on the medal platform.” Smith donated them, saying, “I wanted to give back and let the people know I wasn’t the only one under the pressures of inequality.” (Gift of Tommie and Delois Smith; warm-up suit object no. 2015.231.2, marked public-domain/Open Access.)
- *Where to find it:* NMAAHC collection (search “Tommie Smith”): <https://nmaahc.si.edu/> ; documented in Smithsonian/Seattle Times coverage: <https://www.seattletimes.com/nation-world/the-black-power-salute-that-rocked-the-world-50-years-ago/>
- *Visual grammar:* The garment itself becomes a venerated relic — proof that a worn object can outlast and outweigh the event. Lesson: design the kit as a future artifact.

**3. The “Victory Salute” statue (Rigo 23, 2005)**

- *Description:* 22-foot statue at San Jose State University recreating the podium; Norman’s spot deliberately left empty with a “Take a Stand” plaque so viewers can stand in (Norman himself requested this).
- *Creator/year:* Artist Rigo 23, 2005.
- *Where to find it:* Documented via Wikipedia (<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_Olympics_Black_Power_salute>) and Getty Images SJSU statue photos.
- *Visual grammar:* The empty slot — designed participation. Lesson: leave a deliberate gap/blank in the design that invites the wearer/viewer to complete it.

**4. Colin Kaepernick kneeling (Michael Zagaris, Sept 1, 2016 — first kneel)**

- *Description:* Kaepernick and Eric Reid kneel during the anthem at Qualcomm Stadium, San Diego, Sept 1, 2016 — the first kneel (after sitting in earlier games), done in consultation with Green Beret Nate Boyer, who stands beside them with hand over heart.
- *Creator/year:* Michael Zagaris, 2016.  Marcio Jose Sanchez/AP shot the widely-circulated Sept 12, 2016 frame.
- *Where to find it:* NMAAHC object page, with CC0 usage conditions: <https://nmaahc.si.edu/object/nmaahc_2024.5> ; Getty: <https://www.gettyimages.com/photos/colin-kaepernick-kneel>
- *Visual grammar:* A posture, not a garment, becomes the message; the uniform stays standard while the body dissents. The NMAAHC print is CC0 — usable. Lesson: the protest can be in how the kit is worn/used.

**5. NBA “I Can’t Breathe” warmup shirts (Dec 2014)**

- *Description:* LeBron James, Kyrie Irving, Kevin Garnett, Derrick Rose, Lakers players and others wore black “I Can’t Breathe” warmup tees referencing Eric Garner’s last words after the grand-jury decision not to indict.
- *Creator/year:* Player-driven, December 2014. Key photo: Kathy Willens/AP (LeBron warmup, Dec 8, 2014); Al Bello/Getty.
- *Where to find it:* TIME: <https://time.com/3624684/lebron-james-i-cant-breathe-eric-garner/> ; CNN: <https://www.cnn.com/2014/12/08/justice/protests-grand-jury-chokehold>
- *Visual grammar:* Plain black tee + stark white text worn over/instead of branded warmups — the anti-design. Lesson: a deliberately un-styled garment reads as urgent and authentic against a slick branded backdrop.

**6. Norway national team “Human Rights — On and Off the Pitch” tees (March 2021)**

- *Description:* Per AP (via Sports Illustrated, March 24, 2021), Norway players (incl. Haaland, Ødegaard) lined up “wearing T-shirts over their national-team jerseys that had the message ‘HUMAN RIGHTS’ followed by ‘Respect on and off the pitch’”; earlier warm-up tees read “RESPECT” / “On and off the pitch.” The protest targeted Qatar 2022 migrant-worker conditions before a WC qualifier vs Gibraltar.
- *Creator/year:* Norwegian Football Federation / players, March 24, 2021. Photo: Javier Fergo/AP.
- *Where to find it:* Sky Sports: <https://www.skysports.com/football/news/11095/12255827/> ; CNN: <https://edition.cnn.com/2021/03/25/sport/norway-tshirt-protest-against-qatar-world-cup-spt-intl/index.html>
- *Visual grammar:* Message tee layered OVER the official jersey — literally overwriting the brand surface with a counter-message. Directly transferable to a protest kit concept.

**7. Iran national team silent during anthem (Nov 21, 2022)**

- *Description:* Iran’s players stood silent during their anthem before facing England, in solidarity with the “Woman, Life, Freedom” movement after Mahsa Amini’s death.  (At a September 2022 friendly they had worn black jackets over the national colors and some declined to sing.)
- *Creator/year:* Photos by Julian Finney/Getty and Fadel Senna/AFP, 2022.
- *Where to find it:* NPR: <https://www.npr.org/2022/11/21/1138259076/iran-world-cup-anthem-protest> ; Al Jazeera: <https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/11/21/iran-refuse-to-sing-national-anthem-in-world-cup-opener>
- *Visual grammar:* Silence/absence as protest; covering the official identity with black. Lesson: muting or covering the official identity is itself a statement.

**8. Megan Rapinoe kneeling (Sept 2016)**

- *Description:* Rapinoe knelt during the anthem in 2016 in solidarity with Kaepernick — the first prominent white athlete to do so; US Soccer responded with a “stand respectfully” rule , later rescinded in 2020.
- *Creator/year:* 2016; photo Kevin C. Cox/Getty (Sept 18, 2016, Atlanta).
- *Where to find it:* BuzzFeed News: <https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/mollyhensleyclancy/uswnt-megan-rapinoe-anthem-protest-us-soccer>
- *Visual grammar:* Institutional rules written to suppress a gesture become part of the story. Lesson: document the rule your design “breaks.”

**9. The rules themselves — IOC Rule 50 / FIFA equipment regulations**

- *Description:* Olympic Charter Rule 50.2: “No kind of demonstration or political, religious or racial propaganda is permitted in any Olympic sites, venues or other areas.”  Tightened after the 1968 salute (codified mid-1970s).  FIFA’s disciplinary code similarly bans political messaging on kit.  Recent enforcement: Ukrainian skeleton racer Vladyslav Heraskevych disqualified at the 2026 Winter Games for a “helmet of remembrance.”
- *Where to find it:* CBC: <https://www.cbc.ca/sports/olympics/athlete-protests-remain-banned-tokyo-olympics-1.5996859> ; recent enforcement (Ukraine helmet, 2026): <https://time.com/7377913/>
- *Visual grammar:* The existence of a rulebook policing uniforms is the conceptual frame for the whole project. Lesson: design “against the rulebook” — incorporate the prohibition text itself onto the kit.

### THREAD 2 — BRAND HIJACK / SUBVERTISING / BRANDALISM

**10. Brandalism — London 2012 Olympics “48 Sheet” campaign**

- *Description:* The world’s first collaborative subvertising campaign. Per Wikipedia’s Brandalism entry, “a team of 28 artists installed satirical parodies of corporate advertisements over 36 large-format billboards in Leeds, Manchester, Birmingham, Bristol and London over a five-day period in July 2012,” timed to the London Olympics’ “brand-mania.” Works appropriated Nike, Foot Locker, JD Sports and McDonald’s — including Bill Posters’ “Just Loot It” Nike/Wayne Rooney parody. Artists incl. Robert Montgomery, Ron English, Paul Insect.
- *Creator/year:* Brandalism collective (co-founded by Bill Posters), July 2012.
- *Where to find it:* Wikipedia: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandalism> ; Fast Company: <https://www.fastcompany.com/1681241/> ; brandalism.org.uk
- *Visual grammar:* Perfect mimicry of official ad format + inverted message. Lesson: replicate FIFA/sponsor visual language exactly, then twist the payload.

**11. Brandalism — COP21 Paris 2015 fake ads**

- *Description:* 600 fake posters by 82 artists from 19 countries installed in JCDecaux bus-stop frames spoofing COP21 sponsors   (Air France “part of the problem,” Volkswagen, Engie/GDF-Suez).
- *Creator/year:* Brandalism, Nov–Dec 2015. Artists incl. Escif, Jimmy Cauty, Paul Insect, Kennard Phillips, Revolt Design.
- *Where to find it:* Colossal: <https://www.thisiscolossal.com/2015/11/brandalism-fake-ads-paris/> ; The Conversation: <https://theconversation.com/why-artists-installed-600-fake-adverts-at-cop21-51925> ; brandalism.org.uk
- *Visual grammar:* Authentic logo + signoff line “part of the problem.” Lesson: a counterfeit sponsor tagline is devastatingly effective.

**12. Adbusters & culture jamming (Vancouver)**

- *Description:* Adbusters Media Foundation — anti-consumerist magazine famous for “subvertisements” (spoof ads), Buy Nothing Day, and originating the Occupy Wall Street call.  Local to the user. Drew on the Situationist tactic of détournement; the term “culture jamming” was introduced to founder Kalle Lasn by writer Mark Dery.
- *Creator/year:* Founded 1989 in Vancouver by Kalle Lasn and Bill Schmalz.
- *Where to find it:* Wikipedia: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adbusters> ; adbusters.org
- *Visual grammar:* Détournement — using a brand’s own polish against it.  Lesson: this is Vancouver’s homegrown protest-design tradition; the project can explicitly cite it as local lineage.

**13. First Things First manifesto (1964 / 2000)**

- *Description:* Ken Garland’s 1964 manifesto calling for a “reversal of priorities” away from consumer advertising;  rewritten as First Things First 2000 and published by Adbusters with 33 signatories.
- *Creator/year:* Ken Garland, 1964;  Adbusters/Jonathan Barnbrook et al., 1999–2000.
- *Where to find it:* Wikipedia: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Things_First_1964_manifesto> ; AIGA Eye on Design: <https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/why-ken-garlands-first-things-first-manifesto-keeps-getting-updated/>
- *Visual grammar:* The designer’s ethical stance as a publishable artifact. Lesson: pair the kit with a short manifesto.

**14. The Yes Men — Dow/BBC Bhopal hoax (2004)**

- *Description:* Andy Bichlbaum, posing as “Jude Finisterra” of Dow Chemical on BBC World, announced Dow would compensate Bhopal victims — classic “identity correction.” Per Wikipedia’s The Yes Men entry, “In Frankfurt, Dow’s share price fell 4.24 percent in 23 minutes, wiping $2 billion off its market value” before recovering.
- *Creator/year:* The Yes Men (Bichlbaum & Bonanno), Dec 3, 2004.
- *Where to find it:* The Yes Men: <https://theyesmen.org/project/dowbbc> ; Wikipedia: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Yes_Men>
- *Visual grammar:* Impeccable corporate impersonation to force a “dream” headline. Lesson: a fake official FIFA announcement/kit can voice the truth the institution won’t.

**15. Anti-FIFA street art — Paulo Ito’s crying child (2014)**

- *Description:* Mural on a São Paulo schoolhouse door: a starving, weeping child with only a soccer ball on his plate — the World Cup’s first viral protest image (shared 40,000+ times on Facebook within a week).
- *Creator/year:* Paulo Ito, May 2014 (his own Flickr: <https://www.flickr.com/photos/pauloito/13998946669> ); AP photo of the mural by Andre Penner.
- *Where to find it:* Slate: <https://slate.com/culture/2014/05/paulo-ito-world-cup-a-brazilian-street-artist-has-created-the-world-cup-s-first-viral-image.html> ; NPR: <https://www.npr.org/2014/07/04/328515634/>
- *Visual grammar:* The soccer ball recoded as a symbol of misplaced priorities. Lesson: take the sport’s central object and make it indict the spectacle.

**16. Brazil 2014 “FIFA Go Home” graffiti & protest**

- *Description:* Walls across Rio and São Paulo with “FIFA go home,” “Need Food not Football,” and vandalized mascot/Neymar imagery during the 2014 World Cup protests.
- *Creator/year:* Various street artists/collectives (e.g., OPNI), 2014; photos by Reuters (Sergio Moraes, Nacho Doce) and AFP (Yasuyoshi Chiba, Nelson Almeida).
- *Where to find it:* Getty: <https://www.gettyimages.com/photos/brazil-world-cup-protest> ; Wikipedia: <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2014_protests_in_Brazil>
- *Visual grammar:* Blunt anti-brand typography over civic surfaces. Lesson: a stenciled “go home” register reads instantly.

**17. Amnesty International “Qatar World Cup of Shame” campaign**

- *Description:* Amnesty’s migrant-worker campaigns around Qatar 2022, including “World Cup of Shame” (2016, exposing exploitation at Khalifa International Stadium) and the “Pay Up” coalition (2022).
- *Creator/year:* Amnesty International, 2016–2023.
- *Where to find it:* <https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/campaigns/2016/03/qatar-world-cup-of-shame/>
- *Visual grammar:* NGO co-opting the tournament’s own branding cadence. Lesson: a “World Cup of ___” lockup is a ready counter-brand format.

### THREAD 3 — VANCOUVER STREET CULTURE & PUBLIC SPACE RECLAMATION

**18. China Creek Skatepark — City of Vancouver Archives slides**

- *Description:* Photographs of the China Creek Skateboard Park (two bowls, the “Teacup” and “Bathtub,” built 1979 on East Broadway/Clark Dr — Vancouver’s first public skatepark, built atop a filled-in creek/landfill, which caused the famous concrete cracking). Five slides show the bowls, graffiti, and skaters.
- *Creator/year:* Vancouver Board of Parks and Recreation; dated [between 1970 and 1987].  Catalogue nos. CVA 392-1800.315 through .319 (Slides subseries, reference prefix VPK-S625-2).
- *Where to find it:* City of Vancouver Archives, searcharchives.vancouver.ca (search “China Creek Skateboard Park” or the catalogue numbers). The AtoM platform is JavaScript-rendered, so search rather than deep-link. These are restricted-access slides (freezer storage, 48-hr notice; reproduction request needed).
- *Visual grammar:* Raw, cracked DIY concrete; graffiti as territorial marking. Lesson: an aesthetic of decay and self-built space — the opposite of corporate stadium polish.

**19. 1987 “Save China Creek” protest photos (Vancouver Sun / Province)**

- *Description:* Skateboarders protesting City Council’s 1987 move to close the park.
- *Creator/year:* Ian Smith/Vancouver Sun (May 7, 1987, “Skateboard enthusiasts let their sentiments be known about the possible closure”); Rick Loughran/The Province (June 8, 1987, “David Hakkarainen works out his $300 skateboard”); Carlos Longo at China Creek 1987 (photo by P.D./Skull Skates, private collection).
- *Where to find it:* Reproduced in Vancouver Is Awesome’s 40th-anniversary feature: <https://www.vancouverisawesome.com/events-and-entertainment/china-creek-skatepark-40th-anniversary-vancouver-1944663> ; originals most likely in the Sun/Province negative collections at the City of Vancouver Archives (~2 million press images acquired 2018). Copyright remains with Postmedia even where negatives are archived.
- *Visual grammar:* Grassroots civic protest imagery — kids vs. City Hall over public space. Lesson: skate culture’s fight for space mirrors the kit project’s claim on cultural space.

**20. The fight to legalize street skateboarding in Vancouver**

- *Description:* Vancouver’s Street and Traffic By-law (s.77/77A) banned skateboarding on streets (“No person shall coast or slide with… skateboard… on any street, lane, or other public place”). A December 2001 trial relaxed it on “minor streets”; an April 2003 amendment made it permanent and removed police authority to seize boards — cited by the Vancouver Skateboard Coalition as a North American first. Skateboarding was allowed in bike lanes in 2015.
- *Creator/year:* City of Vancouver; Vancouver Skateboard Coalition (incl. Michael Gordon); Council supporters Jim Green and Suzanne Anton.
- *Where to find it:* City of Vancouver Council report RTS 04887 (2005): <https://council.vancouver.ca/20050315/a8.htm> ; Michael Gordon, Viewpoint Vancouver (2022): <https://viewpointvancouver.ca/2022/07/12/michael-gordon-making-skateboarding-legal/>
- *Visual grammar:* Public space as contested civic territory; the bylaw text itself as raw material. Lesson: ground the project in the local politics of who is allowed to use the street. (Note: “legalization year” is reported as both 2001 trial and 2003 permanent — both correct depending on framing.)

**21. China Creek heritage recognition & Scout Magazine history**

- *Description:* China Creek designated #92 on the Vancouver Heritage Foundation’s “Places That Matter” (July 16, 2022);  Scout Magazine’s local history of the site and its punk/DIY East Van identity (Skull Skates, Peter “P.D.” Ducommun).
- *Where to find it:* Vancouver Heritage Foundation: <https://placesthatmatter.ca/location/china-creek-skatepark-bowls/> ; Scout Magazine: <https://scoutmagazine.ca/2012/02/10/you-should-know-about-the-history-of-east-vancouvers-china-creek-skate-park/>
- *Visual grammar:* Punk/DIY East Van vernacular (Skull Skates lettering, hand-built ethos). Lesson: a local underground brand language to draw from authentically.

### THREAD 4 — LOCAL AUTHENTICITY FIGHTS / WHOSE CULTURE GETS PACKAGED

**22. The Cowichan sweater controversy (2010 Vancouver Olympics)**

- *Description:* Hudson’s Bay Company sold a “$350 hand-knit Olympic sweater featuring a maple leaf and elk” (CBC/Global News) as official merchandise instead of authentic hand-knit Cowichan sweaters by Coast Salish knitters. Cowichan Tribes general manager Ernest “Ernie” Elliott called it a knockoff: “We believe they are not very good knock-offs of the genuine Cowichan sweater. Anything that tries to resemble a genuine Cowichan sweater is a fake.” HBC defended it as “a contemporary design inspired by a great fashion icon… recognized as a knit sweater all across the country.” After the threat of a silent “sweater protest” during the torch relay through Duncan (Oct 31, 2009) and sustained negative press, HBC agreed to sell genuine Cowichan sweaters at Olympic pavilions and its flagship store — though on poor terms (knitter pay offered rose from $95 to $195 per sweater, per councillor Dora Wilson; genuine sweaters retailed ~$400 vs. the Bay’s $350 look-alike, per the Globe and Mail).
- *Creator/year:* Controversy 2009–2010; key figures — Ernest “Ernie” Elliott (Cowichan Tribes GM), Chief Lydia Hwitsum, councillor/knitter Dora Wilson.
- *Where to find it:* CBC: <https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/british-columbia/cowichan-tribes-reach-olympic-sweater-deal-1.830116> ; Globe and Mail: <https://www.theglobeandmail.com/news/national/cowichan-tribes-bay-reach-deal-on-sweaters/article4303529/> ; academic — Regan Shrumm, “Knitting for Our Lives,” ARTiculate (2017): <https://journals.uvic.ca/index.php/articulate/article/view/18872>
- *Visual grammar:* The knockoff vs. authentic garment — the exact tension of the kit project. Lesson: a sweater/kit can be the battleground over who profits from culture. This is the project’s single closest precedent.

**23. The Ilanaaq logo (2010 Olympics) — Inukshuk appropriation debate**

- *Description:* The Vancouver 2010 emblem “Ilanaaq” (“friend” in Inuktitut), a five-piece multicolored inukshuk, drew criticism:  the inukshuk is an Inuit symbol with no tie to Coast Salish/West Coast peoples on whose land the Games sat. Former Nunavut commissioner Peter Irniq noted that a figure with head/arms/legs is properly an “inunnguaq,” not an inukshuk. BC chiefs Stewart Phillip (“remarkable resemblance… to Pac-Man”) and Edward John called it unrepresentative of the region.
- *Creator/year:* Designed by Elena Rivera MacGregor (Rivera Design Group), unveiled April 23, 2005,  chosen from 1,600+ entries.
- *Where to find it:* SFU IPinCH: <https://www.sfu.ca/ipinch/outputs/blog/appropriation-month-ilanaaq-logo-2010-vancouver-olympic-games/> ; CBC: <https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/vancouver-olympic-emblem-comes-under-fire-1.557252>
- *Visual grammar:* A sacred functional marker flattened into a friendly mascot/logo. Lesson: shows how mega-events strip context from Indigenous form — a critique the kit can foreground.

**24. The broader appropriation pattern at the 2010 Games**

- *Description:* Critiques document the wider pattern — the inukshuk reduced to a cartoon logo; imported “authentic aboriginal art” sold at the Games edging out genuine First Nations work — of Indigenous imagery commodified in sports branding.
- *Where to find it:* Ouno Design: <https://ounodesign.com/2009/12/21/the-cowichan-sweater-of-vancouver-island/> ; National Geographic: <https://www.nationalgeographic.com/culture/article/100212-vancouver-2010-winter-olympics-logo-ilanaaq>
- *Visual grammar:* Lesson: as a settler artist, the project should center Indigenous design sovereignty rather than reproduce the extraction it critiques.

## Recommendations

1. **Anchor the collection in two local precedents** — the Cowichan sweater knockoff and Adbusters — since both are Vancouver/BC stories that make the project locally legible and ethically grounded. Lead with the Cowichan case; it is the closest precedent in the entire library.
1. **Borrow the “perfect mimicry, inverted payload” technique** from Brandalism/Yes Men: reproduce FIFA/sponsor visual systems exactly (typography, lockups, sponsor bars) and twist the message (“part of the problem” / “World Cup of Shame”). This is the most reliably powerful single tactic in the corpus.
1. **Load garment details with coded meaning** the way the 1968 protest did (socks, beads, scarf) rather than relying on slogans — subtler, more durable, and harder for a rulebook to police.
1. **Use rights-clean sources where possible:** the NMAAHC Kaepernick print is CC0 and Smith’s tracksuit (object 2015.231.2) is Open Access; Paulo Ito’s own Flickr exists. For agency images (Getty/AP/AFP/Popperfoto), treat them as reference only and recreate/illustrate rather than reproduce.
1. **As a settler artist, foreground Indigenous design sovereignty:** cite, don’t reproduce, Cowichan/Coast Salish motifs; the critique is about extraction, so the work must avoid repeating it. If any Indigenous motif is desired, collaborate with and credit/compensate Coast Salish artists.
1. **Benchmark to change course:** if the project moves from reference/homage toward reproducing protected Indigenous designs or copyrighted agency photos in sellable merch, stop and seek licensing/permission — that threshold is exactly where the project would replicate the harm it critiques.

## Caveats

- Most athlete-protest photographs are held by commercial agencies (Getty, AP, AFP, Popperfoto); the URLs here point to provenance, not free-use licenses. Reuse requires clearance. The clear exceptions are the Smithsonian NMAAHC Open Access / CC0 items.
- City of Vancouver Archives China Creek slides are restricted-access (freezer storage, 48-hour notice); usable digital files require a formal reproduction request, and the Sun/Province negatives remain under Postmedia copyright.
- “Legalization year” for Vancouver street skating is reported as both 2001 (trial) and 2003 (permanent); both are correct depending on framing.
- Some links are secondary (news, blogs); where possible the academic/archive/government original is cited alongside (e.g., ARTiculate for Cowichan, SFU IPinCH for Ilanaaq, City of Vancouver Council report for the skate bylaw).
- The Smithsonian, NMAAHC and City of Vancouver Archives platforms are search-driven; if a deep link does not resolve, search the object/catalogue number named above.
